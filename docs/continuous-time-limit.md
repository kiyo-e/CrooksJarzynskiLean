# Manuscript note: continuous-time limit of the work conventions

## Statement

Let a path on a uniform partition of `[0,T]` be written as
`x₀, …, x_N`, with mesh size `h = T/N`, and let the energy schedule be
`E₀, …, E_N`. Define the edgewise energy increment

```text
qᵢ(x) = Eᵢ₊₁(x) - Eᵢ(x).
```

The quench-then-transition convention evaluates `qᵢ` at `xᵢ`, whereas the
transition-then-quench convention evaluates it at `xᵢ₊₁`. Their difference is

```text
D_N = Σᵢ [qᵢ(xᵢ₊₁) - qᵢ(xᵢ)].
```

The formalization proves the discrete summation-by-parts identity

```text
D_N = q_{N-1}(x_N) - q₀(x₀)
      + Σ_{i=1}^{N-1} [q_{i-1}(xᵢ) - qᵢ(xᵢ)].
```

Consequently, if the schedule satisfies the path-independent finite-difference
bounds

```text
|qᵢ(x)| ≤ M h,
|qᵢ(x) - qᵢ₋₁(x)| ≤ L h²,
```

for every admissible time index and every state, then every path satisfies

```text
|D_N| ≤ 2 M h + (N - 1) L h²
      ≤ (2 M + L T) h
      = T (2 M + L T) / N.
```

The right-hand side is independent of the path and of its probability law.
Hence the two discrete work conventions converge to one another uniformly over
all paths as `N → ∞`.

## Interpretation

This result resolves the ordering ambiguity that is visible at finite time
step: the quench-then-transition and transition-then-quench definitions are
different at fixed discretization, but they approximate the same continuous-time
work under a temporally regular driving schedule.

No path continuity, finite-jump assumption, Markov generator, or stochastic
calculus is used. The state increments disappear after discrete summation by
parts, so the estimate depends only on temporal finite differences of the
energy schedule.

This is not a continuous-time Crooks theorem. It is also not an
Itô–Stratonovich conversion statement: the work considered here is the
finite-variation contribution generated by changing the external energy
schedule in time.

The mechanized theorem assumes the displayed finite-difference bounds directly.
Uniform `C²` bounds in time provide these hypotheses by the ordinary mean-value
estimates; formalizing that analytic corollary is intentionally separated from
the probability-free core theorem.
